Automated textbook generation with large language models typically allocates a
flat word budget per chapter or per concept, independent of how important each
concept is to the rest of the curriculum. We report an informal but consistent
observation across two AI-assisted textbook projects: chapter content quality,
judged subjectively, improved when the word budget for each concept was scaled
by the concept's in-degree in the course's learning graph -- the number of
other concepts that directly depend on it. High-in-degree concepts received
more elaboration: more worked examples, more contrasting cases, and more
metaphors, mirroring practices recommended by elaboration theory and cognitive
load theory but never tied to an explicit graph metric.

We formalize the learning graph as a monopartite concept dependency DAG and
show that raw in-degree undercounts concepts that are foundational only
transitively -- concepts with few direct dependents but many indirect ones. We
propose the Concept Impact Score (CIS), a PageRank-style recursive importance
measure computed over the dependency graph. Because a learning graph is
acyclic by construction, CIS reduces to an exact, closed-form, single-pass
computation over a topological ordering, avoiding the damping-factor
assumptions required for convergence on general (possibly cyclic) graphs such
as the web graph.

We define the chapter-generation problem as three separable sub-problems --
concept-to-chapter partitioning, within-chapter concept ordering, and
per-concept content-budget allocation -- and propose replacing flat or
in-degree-weighted allocation with CIS-weighted allocation in the last of
these. On a real 200-concept, 277-edge Algebra I learning graph, CIS and
in-degree rank concepts very differently: several concepts with in-degree 1-2
(e.g. "Constant", "Coefficient") rank in the top five by CIS because of their
transitive reach. Discretizing CIS into elaboration tiers is not trivial:
an initial population-percentile-rank scheme passed inspection on one
chapter but proved structurally broken on a second (CIS has a hard floor,
and over half the graph's concepts are tied at it, making the bottom tier
unreachable regardless of true importance); we report the fix (a log-scaled
Elaboration Score) and the two-chapter cross-check that exposed the bug, as
a methodological point likely relevant beyond this specific metric. We
describe a planned blind-comparison study, generating matched chapters under
uniform, in-degree-weighted, and CIS-weighted budgets at fixed total chapter
length, rated by both human judges and an LLM-judge rubric, to test whether
CIS-weighted allocation produces higher-quality generated content than the
alternatives.
